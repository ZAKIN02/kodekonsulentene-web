# The StoryBrand Framework: All Seven Elements (2.0 Edition)

How story works: a character who wants something encounters a problem before they can get it. At the peak of their despair, a guide steps into their lives, gives them a plan, and calls them to action. That action helps them avoid failure and experience success.

Two facts about the human brain drive everything: (1) people scan for information that helps them survive and thrive, and (2) the brain conserves calories by ignoring anything confusing. Therefore: communicate simply and clearly how you help customers survive and thrive. If you confuse, you lose.

For each element below: principle, coaching questions (for guided mode), quality bar, and mistakes to avoid.

**Guided-session rule: park misplaced answers.** Users often give an answer that belongs to a different element (e.g., "they want trust" during Element 1, when trust belongs to the Guide in Element 3). Acknowledge it, explicitly park it ("that's gold, and it's actually your Element 3 material; I'll hold it"), and use it when you arrive there. Never discard it and never let it derail the current element.

## Element 1: A Character

**Principle: The customer is the hero, not your brand.** A story starts when the hero wants something. Define one thing the customer wants and the story opens; this opens a "story gap" between where the customer is and where they want to be.

Coaching questions:
- Who is your customer? (One primary character. Resist serving everyone.)
- What do they want, as it relates to your brand? (Brainstorm many, pick ONE.)
- What aspirational identity do they want? Who do they want to become, or how do they want to be perceived by others? (Brands that participate in their customer's transformation win.)

Quality bar:
- The desire must be singular and survival-relevant (saving money, gaining status, saving time, belonging to a tribe, accumulating resources, finding meaning).
- It must be concrete enough to visualize. "Peace of mind" is weak; "a financial plan that survives a recession" is strong.

Mistakes: defining multiple desires (dilutes the story gap); choosing a desire the brand cannot actually deliver; describing the company's ambition instead of the customer's.

---

## Element 2: Has a Problem

**Principle: A story gets interesting when conflict is defined. Talk about your customers' problems over and over.** Customers are only interested in a brand because they are experiencing a problem. Identifying it clearly draws them toward you.

The three levels (the full Story Gap):
- **External:** the tangible, physical problem. (A used car dealer might rip me off.)
- **Internal:** how the problem makes them FEEL. (I feel intimidated and afraid of being played.) Companies sell solutions to external problems, but customers buy solutions to internal problems. This is the level that drives purchase.
- **Philosophical:** why this is just plain wrong. (Honest people deserve honest deals.) Uses words like "ought" and "shouldn't."

**The Villain (do not skip):** identify the single root cause of all three problems. The villain should be (1) a root cause, (2) relatable, (3) singular, and (4) real. Personify a frustration (confusion, clutter, hidden fees, wasted hours), never a competitor by name.

Coaching questions:
- Who or what is the villain in your customer's story?
- What external problem does the villain cause?
- How does that problem make your customer feel? (Use their words, not industry words.)
- Why is it just plain wrong that they suffer this?

Quality bar: the internal problem must be an emotion or self-doubt stated plainly. Resolving all three levels creates brand evangelists.

Mistakes: listing many problems instead of one per level; stating internal problems that are really external ("they need better software" is not a feeling); melodramatic philosophical claims.

## Element 3: Meets a Guide Who Understands Them

**Principle: Customers aren't looking for another hero. They're looking for a guide.** Heroes in trouble need a Yoda or a Haymitch. The brand never plays the hero.

The guide demonstrates exactly two things (2.0 wording):
- **Empathy:** show you understand and care about their frustration. "We know what it feels like to..." Trust is built through empathy first.
- **Competency** (2.0 replaced "authority" with "competency" to avoid sounding superior to the hero): demonstrate you are capable of solving the problem. Use testimonials, statistics, awards, logos, years of results. A few signals only; overdoing it slips back into hero mode.

Coaching questions:
- What one sentence shows you genuinely understand the customer's frustration?
- What 2-4 proof points demonstrate competency? (Numbers beat adjectives.)

Quality bar: empathy statement names the internal problem from Element 2. Competency evidence is specific and countable. **Never fabricate proof points.** When drafting for a business whose real stats are unknown or unproven (common for new ventures), write bracketed placeholder slots, e.g. "[X] audits completed at [Y]% faster turnaround", and tell the user these are slots to fill with real proof as it accumulates. Lead with whatever verified proof the user actually has, even if it's adjacent (founder track record, certifications, pilot results).

Mistakes: leading with credentials before empathy; long trophy lists; backstory about the founders that doesn't serve the customer's story.

## Element 4: Who Gives Them a Plan

**Principle: Customers trust a guide who has a plan.** A purchase is the first moment of real risk (money, time, respect). A plan reduces perceived risk and alleviates confusion, like placing stepping stones across a creek.

Two plan types:
- **Process plan:** 3 (or at most 4) steps from here to purchase or from purchase to result. "1. Schedule a call. 2. We build your roadmap. 3. Watch your message land."
- **Agreement plan (optional):** a list of promises that counters the customer's specific fears. List the fears, write an agreement that disarms each.

Optionally name the plan to increase perceived value ("The Clarity Roadmap").

Coaching questions:
- What are the 3 steps a customer takes to do business with you and win?
- What fears would stop them, and what can you promise to neutralize each?

Quality bar: steps are actions the CUSTOMER takes, written in plain verbs. Three steps, rarely four, never five.

Mistakes: describing the company's internal process; steps that are vague ("engage with us"); too many steps.

---

## Element 5: And Calls Them to Action

**Principle: Customers do not take action unless they are challenged to take action.** Nobody can read minds. Calls to action must be crystal clear and repeated in the same words everywhere.

Two CTA types:
- **Direct:** the purchase ask. "Buy Now." "Schedule a Call." "Get a Quote." One direct CTA, repeated; the obvious button on the website.
- **Transitional:** the on-ramp that builds trust and reciprocity before purchase: free PDF, webinar, sample, assessment, trial. Good transitional CTAs slowly on-ramp people, position you as the guide, create reciprocity, and stake claim to your territory.

Coaching questions:
- What exactly do you want the customer to DO? (One verb phrase.)
- What free, valuable thing can you offer to earn trust and an email address?

Quality bar: direct CTA is 2-4 words, imperative. Transitional CTA names a concrete asset with a benefit-driven title.

Mistakes: passive CTAs ("Learn More" as the primary button); multiple competing direct CTAs; no transitional CTA at all.

---

## Element 6: That Helps Them Avoid Failure

**Principle: Every human being is trying to avoid a tragic ending.** People are more motivated to avoid loss than achieve gain. Define what is at stake; if nothing can be lost, there is no story.

Coaching questions:
- What 2-3 negative consequences happen if they never engage you?
- Which of these does your customer already lie awake worrying about?

Quality bar: failure is the SALT in the bread recipe. A pinch is required; too much scares customers into disengaging. Two or three concrete consequences, stated matter-of-factly, with truthfulness (only name consequences you genuinely help avoid).

Mistakes: omitting stakes entirely (most common); fear-mongering; abstract stakes ("missed opportunities") instead of concrete ones ("another quarter of leads going to competitors").

---

## Element 7: And Ends in Success

**Principle: Never assume people understand how your brand can change their lives. Tell them.** Cast a specific vision of life after the problem is solved. Be like Kennedy: "We're going to put a man on the moon," not "a highly competitive space program." Exciting, clear, visual.

Three levels of resolution to draw from (close the same gaps opened in Element 2):
- External: the task is done, the result delivered.
- Internal: the frustration is replaced by a feeling (confidence, relief, pride).
- Philosophical: the wrong is made right.

**Identity Transformation (From → To):** great brands obsess about their customer's transformation. Define who the customer becomes: From "overwhelmed and ignored" To "clear, confident, and heard." This pairs with the aspirational identity from Element 1.

Coaching questions:
- Paint the scene: what does the customer's Tuesday look like after working with you?
- How will they FEEL once the internal problem is resolved?
- Complete: our customer transforms from ____ to ____.

Quality bar: success items are specific, visual, and map one-to-one against the problems defined in Element 2.

Mistakes: generic success ("grow your business"); listing features instead of results; forgetting the transformation.

## After the seven elements: The Controlling Idea (2.0)

Once the BrandScript is complete, distill it into a **controlling idea**: the single moral or central theme of the brand's story. Like The Lion King stripped of subplots ("a young lion must confront his usurping uncle and take his rightful place as king"), the controlling idea pinpoints what the brand stands for and how it helps, in one sentence. Every message, from tagline to social post, must reinforce it. Customers decide in seconds; the controlling idea is what survives those seconds.

Formula that works well: [Customer] shouldn't have to [villain/problem]. [Brand] gives them [plan/solution] so they can [success/transformation].

Then compress further into the **one-liner** (see applications.md): Character + Problem + Plan + Success in 1-2 sentences a stranger could repeat. Test: can your ideal customer hear it and think "That's me!"

## Campaign discipline (2.0)

Create, edit, repeat. The BrandScript is not a document; it is a script the whole company performs. The same sound bites appear on the website, in emails, in sales conversations, and on packaging, dozens of times. Teach teams to flag anything "off script."
